CREATURES / BIRDS

Birds

Amami Oshima's laurel forests are home to birds found only on this island and its neighbors — from the deep blue Lidth's Jay to the once-legendary Amami Thrush. Many migrants and winter visitors pass through too, so the birds you meet change with the season.

ABOUT

About This Field Guide

Amami Oshima is home to everything from endemic species like Lidth's Jay and the Amami Thrush, found nowhere else, to migratory birds like the Ruddy Kingfisher and the Grey-faced Buzzard that visit only in certain seasons. Birds are grouped here by their closest relatives.

There are many kinds of birds, and new records keep being added, so species are added to this field guide gradually as photos and information come together. Click a photo or name to see that species' full page.

Many of the birds introduced here are protected as national Natural Monuments or Nationally Rare Wild Species, and their capture, collection, or transfer is prohibited by law. Wild birds aren't something to catch and take home — they're best enjoyed by observing and photographing them without disturbing them. Keep a good distance, and avoid using playback calls or food to lure them in. Parent birds and chicks are especially sensitive while nesting or breeding, and approaching too closely or making noise can cause the nest to be abandoned or the chicks to fail to fledge successfully. Avoid flash or strong lights when photographing at night, and don't share information online that could reveal a nest's exact location.
FIELD GUIDE

Birds

―― Passerines (Flycatchers, Crows & Paradise Flycatchers) ――

Ryukyu Robin
Old World flycatcher family (Muscicapidae), genus Larvivora. Larvivora komadori

About 14 cm long, weighing about 18 g. A species endemic to Japan that breeds from the Danjo Islands through the Tokara Islands, Amami Oshima and its surrounding islets, to Tokunoshima; designated a Natural Monument in 1970. It is a year-round resident on Amami Oshima, but males sing especially often to proclaim territory during the breeding season from March to June, so following the song early in the morning is the best way to find one at that time of year. Males have a vivid orange-red head, back, and tail, and the black patches on the breast and flanks look like a "beard," which gives the species its Japanese name. Females are overall plainer in color. It forages for insects on the forest floor of evergreen broadleaf forest. A different species lives in northern Okinawa Island, but the two ranges do not overlap.

Capture and transfer prohibited (Natural Monument)
Amami Thrush
Old World flycatcher family (Muscicapidae), genus Zoothera. Zoothera dauma major

About 30 cm long. A resident bird found only on Amami Oshima, with a yellowish-brown ground color marked with black, tiger-like blotches. Designated a national Natural Monument and a Nationally Rare Wild Species. It prefers the dim forest floor of mature broadleaf forest and feeds on earthworms and the like. During the breeding season from February to early April, it sings a clear, beautiful "kyoroon, tsi-i" call in the dim light before dawn and at dusk. It once declined so sharply due to logging and the impact of invasive species that it was called a "phantom bird," but as the forest has recovered, its population has been on a recovering trend in recent years.

Capture and transfer prohibited (Natural Monument)
Lidth's Jay
Crow family (Corvidae), genus Garrulus. Garrulus lidthi

About 38 cm long. A species endemic to Japan found only on Amami Oshima, Kakeromajima, and Uke-jima, beautiful with its azure-blue body, reddish-brown wings, and white bill and wingtips. Designated a Natural Monument in 1921. It prefers evergreen broadleaf forest and is omnivorous, feeding on chinquapin and oak acorns. Nest-building begins around December, and during the breeding season from around February through May it lays 3-7 eggs in a tree hollow or similar site; late February through early April, when parent birds move about actively near the nest, is an especially good time to spot one. Its numbers once declined due to predation by feral cats and the introduced small Indian mongoose, but conservation efforts have paid off — including the 2024 declaration of mongoose eradication — and its population has been recovering in recent years. It flies about actively during the day, but at night it can be observed resting on power lines or branch tips along forest roads.

Capture and transfer prohibited (Natural Monument)
Ryukyu Paradise Flycatcher
Monarch flycatcher family (Monarchidae), genus Terpsiphone. Terpsiphone atrocaudata illex

Males measure about 45 cm including the tail streamers, females about 18 cm. The whole body has a black-purple sheen, and the eye-ring and bill are a vivid cobalt blue. Males have tail feathers nearly three times the length of the body, giving them a striking silhouette in flight. On Amami Oshima it arrives as a summer migrant around April and, in the dim interior of the forest, flits from branch to branch catching flying insects such as small flies, wasps, and butterflies in mid-air. Its call is said to sound like "tsuki-hi-hoshi, hoi-hoi-hoi" ("moon, sun, stars, hoi-hoi-hoi"), which is why it is named the "three-light bird" after these three lights. From May to July it builds a cup-shaped nest of moss in the fork of a tree and shares incubation and chick-rearing duties. While unfledged chicks are in the nest, lingering close by or shaking the branch can cause the parents to abandon it, so it is best to watch briefly through binoculars from a distance. It heads back to its Southeast Asian wintering grounds around September.

No special protected status

―― Coraciiformes (Kingfishers) ――

Common Kingfisher
Kingfisher family (Alcedinidae), genus Alcedo. Alcedo atthis bengalensis

About 17 cm long and weighing 19-40 g, a small kingfisher. The head and back have a glossy blue-green sheen and the underparts are a vivid orange, earning it the nickname "jewel of the water's edge." On Amami Oshima it is a year-round resident found along rivers, irrigation channels beside rice paddies, and ponds of many kinds. It watches the water from a perch on an overhanging branch or post, then plunges in to catch small fish or shrimp as soon as it spots one. Strongly territorial, it lives alone or in pairs and digs a tunnel nest into a riverbank or eroded slope. The breeding season runs from around March to August, and pairs may raise more than one brood; courtship includes the male passing a small fish to the female, known as courtship feeding, and the birds are especially active and easy to watch during this period. Unlike most of the birds on this site, it can be watched by daylight at the water's edge rather than in the night forest.

No special protected status
Ryukyu Ruddy Kingfisher
Kingfisher family (Alcedinidae), genus Halcyon. Halcyon coromanda bangsi

About 27 cm long, its whole body a glossy reddish-brown, with a pale-blue patch on the rump that stands out clearly in flight. Locally on Amami Oshima it is also known as "Kukkaru," and is loved for its far-carrying "kyororororo" call. It is a summer migrant that arrives from its Southeast Asian wintering grounds around the end of April, near Japan's Golden Week holidays, and heads south again by around September. Despite belonging to the kingfisher family, it eats relatively little fish, preying widely on crabs, frogs, lizards, and insects of the forest. During the breeding season from June to July it lays around five eggs in a tree hollow or an old woodpecker nest, and the chicks fledge around July. Parent birds become especially nervous during this period; approaching the nest tree, making noise, or using flash photography can cause them to abandon the nest or force the chicks to fledge early, so it is best to watch quietly from a distance and for only a short time.

No special protected status

―― Owls ――

Ryukyu Scops Owl
Owl family (Strigidae), genus Otus. Otus elegans elegans

About 22 cm long, one of the smallest owls found in Japan. The whole body is brown with fine, moth-eaten-looking mottling. It is a year-round resident found from Amami Oshima southward through the Ryukyu Islands, occurring not only in mature forest but also in woodland near houses. Nocturnal, after sunset it repeats a low "koho, koho" call, and the female sometimes answers with a "nya." It preys on insects, spiders, and geckos. It nests in tree hollows, old woodpecker holes, or among the fronds of bird's-nest fern, and lays 4-5 eggs during the breeding season from April to around July. Only the female incubates, but both parents share the work of feeding the chicks, and most fledge around July. Around the time of fledging, chicks are still unsteady in flight and may be found perched on branches close to the ground; rather than picking them up or shining a light on them for long periods, it is best to quietly leave the area. The call itself can be heard year-round, not only during the breeding season.

IUCN Red List: Near Threatened (NT)

―― Hawks ――

Grey-faced Buzzard
Hawk family (Accipitridae), genus Butastur. Butastur indicus

Males are about 47 cm long and females about 51 cm, with a wingspan of 105-115 cm; a medium-sized hawk. The head is grayish-brown, the upperparts and breast are brown, and the throat is white with a black central streak. It preys mainly on small animals such as snakes, lizards, and frogs, as well as insects like cicadas and grasshoppers. It breeds in Kyushu and northward through Honshu and Shikoku, and once breeding is finished, birds migrate south from early September through the Nansei Islands toward Southeast Asia. Amami Oshima is known as Japan's largest wintering ground for the species, with the passage peaking around mid-October, when large numbers of birds can be seen circling and rising together in what is called a "hawk pillar." Many birds do not complete the journey south and instead winter on the island; an estimated 2,000 or so stay from November through around March. It is listed as Vulnerable on the Ministry of the Environment's Red List and on Appendix II of CITES. Around the forests used as roosts, avoid loud noises, sudden movements, and flash photography that could startle the birds into unnecessary flight, and observe quietly from a distance.

Ministry of the Environment Red List: Vulnerable (VU)

―― Woodpeckers ――

Owston's Woodpecker
Woodpecker family (Picidae), genus Dendrocopos. Dendrocopos leucotos owstoni

About 28 cm long, a medium-sized woodpecker. An endemic subspecies found only on Amami Oshima; the upper body is almost entirely black with white spots, and the underside is a stronger brown than in other subspecies. The crown is red in males and black in females, making the sexes easy to tell apart. It lives mainly in mature laurel forest and excavates nest holes in the trunks of large broadleaf trees. It feeds mainly on longhorn beetle larvae hidden in rotten wood. Territorial proclamations and courtship displays begin around winter, and from February through May its "drumming" — rapid pecking at a trunk with its bill — and calls are easiest to hear. In recent years its range has expanded across the whole of Amami Oshima, partly due to the effects of pine wilt disease. Since the only other woodpecker on Amami Oshima is the Amami pygmy woodpecker, the two can be told apart by size alone. Designated a national Natural Monument in 1971 and a Nationally Rare Wild Species in 1993; capture and transfer are prohibited.

Capture and transfer prohibited (Natural Monument / Nationally Rare Wild Species)

―― Pigeons ――

Ryukyu Whistling Green-Pigeon
Pigeon family (Columbidae), genus Treron. Treron permagnus

About 35 cm long, somewhat larger than the White-bellied Green-Pigeon, and a year-round resident. Its whole body is a vivid green. The Japanese name, meaning "red-headed green-pigeon," comes from the reddish head of its close relative the Taiwan Whistling Green-Pigeon (Treron formosae), but this species, found on Amami Oshima and elsewhere, is green even on the head. It lives in laurel forest from lowlands to mountains and feeds in the trees on fruit and seeds, including that of Ficus erecta. It gives a distinctive shakuhachi-like call, "oo-ao, pe, hepo." It breeds around May, laying two eggs. Due to habitat fragmentation and decline, it is listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List.

IUCN Red List: Near Threatened (NT)

―― Shorebirds (Sandpipers & Plovers) ――

Amami Woodcock
Sandpiper family (Scolopacidae), genus Scolopax. Scolopax mira

About 35-36 cm long. A species endemic to Japan found only in the Amami and Okinawa island groups, with breeding confirmed on Amami Oshima, Kakeromajima, Uke-jima, Yoro-jima, and Tokunoshima. It resembles the closely related Eurasian woodcock but has longer legs, an overall blacker tone, and a stockier build than other sandpipers. It prefers the dim forest floor of evergreen broadleaf forest, is most active from dusk to dawn, and forages on the ground for earthworms and insects. It becomes especially active in the dim light of early morning and evening, and on moonlit nights. The breeding season runs from around February to May, and at dusk males perform a courtship and territorial "roding" display flight, making this the easiest time of year to spot one. It nests on the ground within forests and lays 2-4 eggs from around March through May. Designated a Nationally Rare Wild Species in 1993. Predation by mongooses and feral cats, along with habitat loss from logging, are ongoing concerns.

Capture and transfer prohibited (Nationally Rare Wild Species)
Northern Lapwing
Plover family (Charadriidae), genus Vanellus. Vanellus vanellus

About 32 cm long, with a long crest on the head, a back with a glossy greenish-black sheen, and a white belly. Its elegant appearance has earned it the nickname "winter lady." It breeds across Eurasia and visits Japan as a winter migrant. On Amami Oshima it begins to appear in open farmland such as rice paddies and fields from around late October, staying through the winter until around late March or April of the following year. It moves in flocks, foraging in the soil for earthworms and insects. Unlike the creatures of the night forest that this site mainly features, it is a messenger of winter that is easy to observe by day in open farmland.

No special protected status
